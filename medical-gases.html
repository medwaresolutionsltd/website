1. What Are Medical Gases?

Medical gases are gases used for patient treatment, anaesthesia, respiratory support, surgical procedures, medical equipment operation and other healthcare applications. Depending on the facility's clinical services, these gases may be supplied through cylinders, centralised manifolds, on-site generation plants or bulk storage systems connected to a medical gas pipeline system.

Common medical gases used in healthcare facilities include:

  • Medical Oxygen — Supports patients who require supplemental oxygen, including respiratory care, emergency treatment, intensive care and anaesthesia. Hospitals may obtain it through cylinder manifolds, PSA oxygen generating plants or bulk liquid oxygen systems.
  • Nitrous Oxide — Used in selected anaesthesia and pain-management applications. Its supply system must be designed for the intended clinical use and appropriate safety requirements.
  • Medical Air — Used in respiratory care and other clinical applications where medical-quality compressed air is required.
  • Surgical Air — Powers compatible surgical tools and equipment. Its specifications differ from medical air, so the two systems must not be treated as interchangeable.
  • Medical Vacuum — Not a medical gas, but an essential part of hospital medical gas infrastructure. It provides suction for removal of fluids and secretions during clinical procedures.
  • Other Specialised Gases — Carbon dioxide, nitrogen and other gases used for particular surgical, laboratory or diagnostic applications.

The selection of medical gases depends on the services offered by the hospital. A general outpatient clinic, maternity facility, surgical hospital and tertiary referral hospital will not necessarily require the same system configuration.

2. Why Reliable Medical Gas Systems Matter in Hospitals

A dependable medical gas system is a critical part of hospital infrastructure. It connects the supply source to the clinical areas where gases and vacuum are required.

Supporting Patient Care

Medical gas systems provide access to essential gases at designated clinical points, helping healthcare professionals deliver treatment according to clinical requirements.

Reducing Supply Interruptions

Appropriately designed supply systems, backup sources and monitoring arrangements help facilities manage supply changes and equipment failures. Backup capacity should be based on the hospital's demand and risk assessment.

Improving Hospital Efficiency

Centralised medical gas systems reduce the need to move individual cylinders between every point of care. This improves operational efficiency when supported by suitable cylinder-handling procedures, maintenance and supply management.

Supporting Safe Clinical Environments

Gas-specific terminal units, correctly identified pipelines, isolation valves, monitoring equipment and alarm systems help reduce the risk of incorrect connections and make it easier to identify supply problems.

Supporting Hospital Expansion

Medical gas systems can be planned around the facility's current and projected clinical requirements. Early engineering assessment helps hospitals account for additional beds, theatres, ICUs and other clinical departments before expanding their infrastructure.

In Kenya, these considerations are particularly important for facilities that need dependable oxygen delivery and effective management of medical oxygen infrastructure.

3. Common Medical Gas Problems in Kenyan Hospitals

Hospitals can experience medical gas problems arising from undersized systems, ageing infrastructure, equipment faults, poor maintenance or inadequate supply planning. Identifying these challenges early can help prevent avoidable interruptions.

Unreliable Medical Oxygen Supply

Facilities that depend on cylinder deliveries may face logistical challenges when consumption increases or deliveries are delayed.

How to address it: Assess actual oxygen demand, establish suitable primary and backup sources, and develop an effective supply and cylinder-management plan. Depending on the facility, an on-site oxygen generating plant may be appropriate.

Low Pipeline Pressure

Insufficient pipeline capacity, excessive demand, supply equipment faults or restrictions in the distribution network can contribute to low pressure at clinical outlets.

How to address it: Investigate the supply plant, regulators, pipeline capacity, flow demand and pressure readings before determining the corrective action.

Medical Gas Leaks

Leaks can result from defective connections, damaged components, unsuitable installation practices or deterioration over time.

How to address it: Arrange appropriate leak testing, investigate the affected section and have qualified personnel carry out repairs followed by the necessary verification tests.

Faulty Alarm Systems

A malfunctioning alarm can prevent staff from receiving timely warnings about abnormal supply conditions.

How to address it: Inspect, test and maintain the relevant alarm panels, sensors and associated components in accordance with the system design and manufacturer's recommendations.

Inadequate Preventive Maintenance

Failure to maintain compressors, vacuum pumps, oxygen plants, manifolds and other system components can increase the risk of unexpected breakdowns.

How to address it: Establish a documented preventive maintenance plan with defined inspection intervals, service records, fault reporting and corrective actions.

Lack of Backup Supply

A hospital may be exposed to interruptions if its primary source fails and its backup arrangements are inadequate or unavailable.

How to address it: Evaluate the resilience of the complete supply arrangement, including backup capacity, automatic changeover where applicable, alarm functionality, power requirements and emergency procedures.

4. Medical Gas Systems and Their Main Components

A medical gas pipeline system (MGPS) is an integrated arrangement that delivers specified gases and vacuum from supply sources to designated points of use. The system must be designed around the facility's clinical needs, gas requirements, expected consumption and applicable technical standards.

Medical Gas Supply Sources

  • Cylinder manifolds
  • PSA oxygen generating plants
  • Bulk liquid oxygen storage and associated equipment
  • Medical air compressor plants
  • Medical vacuum plants
  • Other specialised gas supply arrangements

The selection of a supply source should consider demand, continuity requirements, operating costs, available space, utilities, maintenance capability and backup arrangements.

Medical Gas Pipelines

Pipelines distribute gases from the supply source to clinical areas. Their material, sizing, installation and identification must be suitable for the intended gas and system design. Medical gas pipelines require appropriate cleanliness and installation controls to minimise contamination and support safe operation.

Area Valve Service Units

Area valve service units allow designated pipeline sections to be isolated when required. Their location and arrangement should support safe access and the hospital's emergency procedures.

Medical Gas Terminal Units

Terminal units provide connection points for compatible clinical equipment. They must be gas-specific and designed to prevent incompatible connections. Correct identification and installation are essential to reducing the risk of gas cross-connection.

Alarm and Monitoring Systems

Alarm systems monitor designated supply or distribution conditions and alert relevant personnel when abnormal conditions occur. Their functionality, locations and monitoring arrangements should be established during system design and verified during commissioning.

Bed Head Units and Medical Pendants

Bed head units and medical pendants can integrate medical gas outlets with electrical services and other clinical connections. Their configuration depends on the room layout, equipment requirements and intended clinical use.

Medical Air and Vacuum Plants

Medical air plants provide compressed air of the required quality, while medical vacuum plants provide suction. Both require suitable capacity, controls, filtration or treatment where applicable, and appropriate maintenance.

5. Medical Gas Specifications: What Should Hospitals Consider?

Medical gas specifications determine how effectively a system meets the hospital's clinical, operational and safety requirements. Specifications should be developed by suitably qualified professionals using the approved design, applicable standards, manufacturer documentation and relevant Kenyan requirements.

Oxygen Supply Capacity

Oxygen plant or manifold capacity should be calculated from the facility's expected demand, including simultaneous consumption, peak clinical requirements and future expansion where appropriate. For oxygen generating plants, also consider required oxygen quality, output pressure, storage capacity, operating conditions, maintenance needs and backup supply.

Medical Air Specifications

Medical air systems should be selected according to the required flow, operating pressure, air quality and intended applications. Compressors, air receivers, dryers, filters, monitoring equipment and associated components should be appropriate for the specified system.

Medical Vacuum Specifications

Medical vacuum plants should be sized according to the number and type of intended users, expected simultaneous demand, required vacuum performance and distribution network. Also assess pump redundancy, receiver capacity, controls and maintenance access.

Pipeline Materials and Sizing

The pipeline design must account for the gas being distributed, the required flow, acceptable pressure losses, installation conditions and applicable standards. Materials and components must be suitable for medical gas service. Correct pipe identification, jointing procedures, cleanliness and installation quality are essential.

Terminal Units and Connections

Each outlet must correspond to the designated gas or vacuum service and be compatible with the intended equipment. The number and location of outlets should reflect the approved room layouts and clinical requirements.

Alarm and Backup Requirements

The system design should define the monitoring points, alarm functions, primary and secondary supply arrangements and response procedures required to support safe operation.

Testing and Commissioning Requirements

Before a new or modified system is placed into service, the required verification should establish that the system has been installed correctly and performs as intended. This can include pipeline integrity and leakage tests, pressure and flow verification, gas identity and quality checks, alarm tests, terminal-unit checks and documentation.

Important: Medical gas specifications are project-specific. Hospitals should not rely on generic pressure, purity, flow or capacity figures without confirming that they apply to the intended gas, equipment and system design.

6. Medical Gases Supply in Kenya: Choosing the Right Solution

Choosing the right medical gases supply arrangement requires an understanding of the facility's clinical demand, available infrastructure, operating environment and continuity requirements. There is no single supply solution that suits every hospital.

Cylinder Manifold Systems

Cylinder manifold systems connect cylinder banks to a regulated supply network. Automatic changeover arrangements can switch between cylinder banks when configured for that purpose.

Suitable for: Smaller facilities, backup supply arrangements and locations where central oxygen generation or bulk storage may not be practical. Hospitals must plan for cylinder availability, storage, handling, replenishment and safe operating procedures.

PSA Oxygen Generating Plants

Pressure swing adsorption plants generate oxygen on-site using an air-processing and separation process.

Suitable for: Facilities with sufficient and reasonably predictable oxygen demand, suitable utilities and infrastructure, and the technical capacity to operate and maintain the plant. Planning should consider the specified oxygen quality, rated output, actual consumption, redundancy, power supply, ventilation and backup arrangements.

Bulk Liquid Oxygen Systems

Bulk liquid oxygen systems store oxygen in specialised tanks and convert it to gaseous form for distribution through the hospital pipeline.

Suitable for: Facilities with appropriate demand, space, delivery access and the infrastructure required to operate a bulk storage system. The installation must account for relevant safety distances, equipment requirements, supply logistics and emergency planning.

A Hybrid Supply Arrangement

Some hospitals may benefit from a combination of primary and secondary supply sources. For example, a facility may use an oxygen generating plant alongside a cylinder manifold or another suitable backup arrangement. The configuration should be determined through engineering assessment and a continuity-of-supply risk review.

7. Medical Gas Pipeline Installation in Kenya

Proper medical gas pipeline installation is essential to the performance and safety of the complete system. Whether the project involves a new hospital, an operating theatre, an ICU, a maternity unit or an extension to an existing facility, the installation process should be based on approved drawings, technical specifications and appropriate quality controls.

Step 1: Site Assessment and Requirements Definition

The project team assesses the facility's clinical services, outlet requirements, supply capacity, plant room, existing infrastructure and proposed expansion. This assessment informs the engineering design and procurement process.

Step 2: System Design and Planning

The design establishes the supply sources, pipeline routes, pipe sizes, valves, terminal units, alarm locations and other required components. Coordination with the architectural, mechanical, electrical and clinical teams helps avoid installation conflicts and costly changes.

Step 3: Equipment and Material Procurement

Equipment and materials should be selected against the approved technical specifications. Procurement should consider quality, compatibility, manufacturer documentation, availability of spare parts and long-term technical support.

Step 4: Pipeline and Equipment Installation

Qualified personnel install the pipelines, manifolds, valves, terminal units, alarms and plant equipment in accordance with the approved design and applicable installation requirements. Proper identification, protection against contamination and inspection of installation work are important throughout the process.

Step 5: Testing and Verification

The completed installation must undergo the required tests and inspections before being placed into clinical service. Verification should address leakage, pressure, flow, gas identity, gas quality, alarm operation and other relevant system functions.

Step 6: Commissioning and Handover

Commissioning confirms that the installed system meets the defined performance and safety requirements. The handover should include relevant test results, equipment manuals, maintenance recommendations, operating instructions and other required documentation.

Step 7: Training and Maintenance Planning

Hospital personnel should receive appropriate orientation on the system, alarm response, isolation procedures and routine operational responsibilities. A planned maintenance programme should be established to support long-term performance.

8. Medical Gas Maintenance, Repair and Safety

Medical gas systems need ongoing maintenance to support safe, reliable performance throughout their service life. Even a properly installed system can develop problems if equipment is not inspected, alarms are not tested or faults are not investigated promptly.

Preventive Maintenance

Preventive maintenance involves planned inspections and servicing of relevant equipment, including oxygen generating plants, manifolds, compressors, vacuum pumps, control panels and alarm systems. Maintenance intervals should follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions, applicable standards and the facility's maintenance plan.

Corrective Maintenance and Repairs

When faults occur, the affected system should be assessed by competent personnel. Corrective action should address the underlying fault, not merely its symptoms. After repairs or modifications, the necessary checks and tests should be completed before the affected section is returned to service.

Leak Detection and Pressure Checks

Appropriate testing helps identify leakage, pressure abnormalities and other system performance issues. Any investigation or repair must follow approved procedures that protect patients and maintain safe supply to unaffected clinical areas.

Alarm and Backup-System Testing

Alarm systems and backup arrangements should be checked according to the system design and maintenance requirements. Hospitals should also ensure that staff understand the actions to take when alarms indicate a supply problem.

Documentation and Maintenance Records

Accurate records help hospital engineers monitor recurring faults, plan replacements and demonstrate that required maintenance and testing have been performed. Records should include relevant inspection findings, corrective actions, test results and recommendations.

10. Medical Gas Standards and Compliance

Medical gas systems must be designed, installed, tested and maintained in accordance with the applicable standards, approved project specifications and relevant Kenyan requirements.

ISO 7396-1 addresses medical gas pipeline systems for compressed medical gases and vacuum, including system design, installation, performance, testing, commissioning and documentation. Requirements for anaesthetic gas scavenging systems are addressed separately under ISO 7396-2.

For each project, the applicable edition of the relevant standard should be confirmed at the design and procurement stage. Hospitals and contractors should also consider relevant Ministry of Health guidance, manufacturer instructions, project-specific requirements and applicable safety procedures.

Compliance is not achieved simply by purchasing equipment described as compliant. The complete installation must be appropriately designed, installed, tested and documented.

Common medical gases include oxygen, nitrous oxide and medical air. Some hospitals also require surgical air, carbon dioxide, nitrogen and other specialised gases. An MGPS is a system comprising supply sources, pipelines, control and monitoring equipment, alarms and terminal units that delivers designated medical gases to clinical areas. A medical gas pipeline system can reduce dependence on moving individual cylinders between points of care, provided the central supply and backup arrangements are properly designed and maintained.

Critical components should be inspected and serviced at defined intervals, with relevant tests, records and corrective actions documented.
